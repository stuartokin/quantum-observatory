The quantum sensor uses cold-atom interferometry to measure acceleration and rotation with high precision. By combining these measurements from a known starting point, a platform can calculate its position throughout a journey without external signals — making it resistant to GPS jamming, spoofing, or signal loss in environments like the High Arctic where satellite coverage is degraded.

The Imperial programme has been running since 2018. A first sea trial aboard the Royal Navy research vessel XV Patrick Blackett followed in 2023. The London Underground deployment tested performance in a GPS-denied urban environment. The December 2025 Arctic trial is explicitly aimed at exposing the device to maritime shock, vibration, and temperature extremes — conditions the lab cannot replicate.

The programme is an early-stage development effort, not a procurement decision. No fleet deployment has been announced, no operational specification published, and no independent performance measurement has been made available. The significance is that a named defence client is funding iterative real-world testing of a quantum technology against a specific operational gap — GPS-denied navigation — rather than waiting for laboratory readiness.
